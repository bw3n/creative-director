# Heritage vs. Relevance

A pattern that has appeared in the corpus more than once. The principle generalises across campaigns.

## What it is

A brand with a heritage identity — old product, established audience, decade-or-longer history — faces an audience that has aged out or moved on. The brand must decide whether to refresh the read on itself, or pretend it never was what it was.

The tension is not "heritage vs. modern." Both can be true at once. The tension is **how the brand treats its own past**: as something to honour, to erase, to update, to escape.

## Why it matters

Heritage brands that err toward erasure lose the audience that loved them. Heritage brands that refuse to update lose the audience they need next. Most rebrand pitches default to erasure because erasure reads as "modern" in the pitch room. The work that survives is the work that finds a third move: facelift, not replacement. Update the read, don't replace the brand.

## When to use it

Apply when:
- The brand has 10+ years of equity.
- The audience has shifted (aged, changed values, moved on culturally).
- The brief asks for "modernisation," "refresh," or "rebrand."
- The agency or in-house team is being asked to "make it feel new."

## When to break it

- The brand has no heritage equity (less than 5 years, no distinct visual or verbal identity, no audience memory). Facelift is a costume without a body.
- The audience shift is so severe that the brand's identity is now actively alienating. Facelift is then a delay. Honest replacement may be the right move.
- The category has genuinely moved past the brand's positioning (e.g. a film-camera brand trying to keep facelift alive in a phone-camera world).

## Worked examples from the corpus

- `schulstad-emergency-slice.md` — Schulstad honoured rye bread's heritage by giving it the form of an emergency care package. The product's heritage status is the reason the reframing works.
- `underground-ice-cream.md` — Underground Ice Cream reframed for calorie-savvy millennials. The agency's stated brief explicitly says "preserve retro charm while reimagining." The facelift framing is in the brief itself.

## Anti-example

A heritage brand that hired a global agency and produced a clean-slate rebrand: new logo, new colour palette, new verbal identity, no reference to the original. The audience reacts with "this isn't the brand I knew." Sales drop in the segment that was loyal; gains in the new segment don't compensate. The brand spends two years trying to recover the original identity.

## The facelift framing, in one sentence

> We are not replacing the brand. We are updating the read on the brand. The product, the visual identity, the verbal register — all of it carries forward. What changes is what the audience notices about it.

If the team can't say this sentence, the brief has slipped toward erasure.
