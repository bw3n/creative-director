## What it was trying to do

Healthcare AI is a category that's either invisible (B2B-only, jargon-heavy, no human face) or over-promised (the AI-cures-everything trope). Aritium needed a campaign that positioned their AIoT pain-measurement technology as both credible and humane — a tool that *sees* what patients can't say, without reducing patients to data points.

## What it actually did

A thermal-imaging diagnostic app + dashboard that visualises pain as a colour-mapped thermal image on the body. The case board shows: a phone-sized device reading the patient's back (a phone displaying a thermal gradient with heat zones marked at 49°F / 50°F), a desktop dashboard with intensity-of-pain score (1–10), sensation-of-pain qualifiers (Dull, Burning, Sharp, Tender, Hot, Aching, Cramping, Throbbing, Tingling, Stiff), a patient-history timeline (Feb 2024 → Mar 2024), and anatomical-pattern visualisations (front/back body outlines with hot-spot markers).

The brand wordmark "Painvisible" is split with a multi-colour thermal-gradient square in place of the "i" — a logo that literally *is* the visual mechanism.

## The tension

**Invisible suffering vs. measurable evidence.** Pain is famously subjective — the patient's word against the doctor's. Aritium's technology makes pain *visible* via thermal imaging, turning a felt-but-unseen condition into a colour-mapped, reproducible, sharable data point.

A second tension sits underneath: **clinical vs. human.** Medical diagnostics usually err cold — numbers, charts, the doctor looking at the screen. Aritium's positioning is that the data is in service of the patient, not the clinician. The campaign reads pain as a *human* event that the technology finally makes *legible*.

A third tension, from the brand name itself: **pain vs. invisible.** Two words in opposition. The campaign doesn't deny either word; it bridges them.

## Why it works (or doesn't)

It works because the visual mechanism IS the insight. You don't need to explain what the technology does — the phone-on-back photograph *shows* it. The patient becomes legible. That's the promise.

The category cliché it escapes: **the AI-cures-everything health campaign.** Most health-tech campaigns promise diagnosis, prediction, or cure in language borrowed from pharma. Painvisible refuses that. It doesn't promise to cure pain. It promises to *see* pain. The promise is to make the patient's experience legible, not to replace it with a verdict. That's a more honest contract with the audience — patients, clinicians, and regulators.

A second cliché escaped: **the abstract data dashboard.** Most health-tech campaign imagery shows clean charts, glowing graphs, and floating numbers. Painvisible shows a *body*. The dashboard is in service of the body, not the other way around. The visual hierarchy inverts the standard health-tech imagery.

## The visual mechanism

The thermal-imaging phone app is the visual mechanism. A real smartphone (or a phone-form-factor device) becomes a thermal camera pointed at the patient; the screen displays a colour-mapped heat gradient with annotations. The colour scale runs from cool blues (low intensity) to warm oranges and reds (high intensity), with markers at specific temperature thresholds.

The case board combines the phone view with a desktop dashboard: a "Mikaela Adu" patient record dated `02·03·2024`, three back readings (`99.2 / 100.3 / 100.1`), zone minima (0.30 / 0.54 / 0.32), delta T (104.3 / 102.7 / 100.8), intensity score, sensation-of-pain checklist, primary-area-of-pain tags, patient-history timeline, body-pattern visualisations, and qualitative prompt ("HOW WAS THERE A SPECIFIC CAUSE OR INCIDENT?" / "DOES THIS PAIN KEEP YOU FROM..." / "WHAT MAKES IT WORSE / BETTER").

The design register is clinical-clean: thin sans-serif type, generous white space, monochrome body photography, thermal colour as the only chromatic accent. The thermal palette IS the brand.

## The typography register

Clinical / medical-instrument typography. Thin sans, all-caps section labels (`INTENSITY OF PAIN`, `SENSATION OF PAIN`, `PRIMARY AREA OF PAIN`), lowercase descriptors, restrained. The aesthetic reads like a hospital-grade diagnostic tool — not a consumer fitness app, not a pharma brand-book. The type says "this is real" by not performing.

## The refusal

Refused: the cheerful health-tech aesthetic. No pastel gradients, no smiling models, no aspirational lifestyle imagery. The campaign's subject is a patient's back, shot in monochrome, with a phone held against it. That's not glamorous. It is, however, the point.

Refused: the diagnostic-as-verdict posture. The dashboard asks questions ("HOW WAS THERE A SPECIFIC CAUSE?") rather than issuing conclusions. The patient is a partner in the diagnosis, not a subject of it.

Refused: AI as autonomous. The tool is positioned as AI-assisted but clinician-mediated and patient-participatory. The agency list (creative director, director, DP, full-stack developer, music producer, post-production, talent agent, VO talent, web creative designer, web developer) makes the human authorship of the technology visible. AI doesn't appear in the credits.

## What to steal

The principle: **the visual mechanism is the insight.** When the technology *is* the visualisation, the campaign doesn't need to explain it. The phone-on-back photograph communicates the entire value proposition. Build the campaign around the artefact, not around the claim.

A second stealable move: **the patient as co-author of the diagnosis.** Painvisible asks the patient "how was there a specific cause?" and "does this pain keep you from…?" The patient's voice is in the tool. That's a stance on what AI in healthcare *should* be — assistive, not autonomous. Most AI-health campaigns position the AI as the diagnostician. Painvisible positions it as the visualiser.

A third: **the logo carries the mechanism.** The "i" in Painvisible is a thermal gradient. The brand wordmark IS the visual claim. Every impression reinforces the insight.

## What not to steal

The clinical-clean typography without the underlying rigour. The register works because the campaign actually has the diagnostic data behind it. A consumer campaign borrowing the aesthetic without the substance reads as hollow. The register is a consequence, not a costume.

The thermal colour palette. It's a specific visual mechanism for a specific technology. Borrowed onto a campaign for a different product (say, a fitness app or a sleep tracker), the palette reads as decoration.

The "patient-as-co-author" stance. It works for a campaign whose underlying product actually asks the patient questions. Don't borrow it as a copy tone unless the product does the same.
