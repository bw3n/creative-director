## What it was trying to do

Pulse oximeters underestimate blood oxygen in people with darker skin tones — a7% measurement error that translates to people being sent home untreated. EarSwitch had solved the engineering problem (move the sensor to the ear canal where melanin doesn't interfere). The brief was to make the medical system and the public *care* about the bias — and to do it without making it a "diversity campaign," which the audience would dismiss.

## What it actually did

A consumer-style product film that frames pulse-oximeter bias as a *design failure*, not a moral one. The campaign refuses testimonials, refuses emotion-led framing, refuses guilt. It presents the problem as a measurement error with measurable consequences — and Airquity as the technical fix. EarSwitch has been accepted into the UK's Innovative Devices Access Pathway (IDAP), and FDA updated guidance in early 2025 requiring greater racial inclusion in clinical testing.

## The tension

**Diversity campaign vs. systems critique.** The default move on racial bias in healthcare is a diversity campaign: testimonials, faces, emotion, "we care." Airquity refuses that. It treats bias as an engineering problem — measurable, fixable, with a real solution. The campaign is a systems critique that happens to involve race.

A second tension sits underneath: **moral framing vs. functional framing.** "Pulse oximeters are racist" is moral. "Pulse oximeters have a7% measurement error on darker skin" is functional. The campaign picks the functional frame. Audiences can't argue with a measurement; they can argue with a moral charge.

## Why it works (or doesn't)

It works because the *evidence* does the persuasion. By refusing emotional framing, the campaign leaves the audience with no argument. The bias is real, it's measurable, the fix exists. There's nothing to push back on.

The category cliché it escapes: **"raise awareness."** The default healthcare-equity move is to ask people to care. Airquity doesn't ask. It shows a measurement problem with a measurement solution.

## The visual mechanism

A consumer-style product film. Clinical but accessible — not a peer-reviewed paper, not a CNN segment. The mechanism is the *register* of the film: it speaks in the visual language of consumer health tech (Apple-style product shots, clean medical aesthetics) but the subject matter is bias and inclusion. The register-mismatch does the work.

The product itself — the oximeter designed for the ear canal — is the visual proof. It's an object that exists. You can see it. You can understand it. The campaign trusts the object to carry the argument.

## The typography register

Clean medical-tech sans-serif. The IDAP and FDA references function as evidence, not decoration. Clinical authority borrowed from peer-reviewed journals, applied to a product film.

## The refusal

Refused: the testimonial. No patients telling their stories. No doctors confessing. No data-dump "shocking" statistic with music swelling underneath.

Refused: the moral charge. The campaign doesn't say "this is wrong" or "we should feel bad." It says "here is the error rate. Here is the fix."

Refused: the awareness framing. The campaign doesn't ask the audience to care more. It shows them a measurement, and trusts the audience to draw the conclusion.

## What to steal

The principle: **when the problem is engineering, the campaign is engineering.** If your brand's insight is a measurement error, a design flaw, a system that fails on a specific dimension — frame the campaign as a *fix*, not as a moral appeal. Functional framing travels further than emotional framing.

A second stealable move: **borrow register, not content.** Airquity is a health-tech startup, but the campaign borrows the visual register of consumer product films (Apple, Beats, etc.). The register does the audience-handling; the content does the work.

A third: **let the object be the proof.** Don't narrate the problem. Show the object that solves it. The oximeter is the campaign. If your brand has a real, visual product, trust it to carry the argument.

## What not to steal

The evidence-led discipline only works if your evidence is *real*. A fabricated 7% measurement error would be a scandal. If you don't have the receipts, the functional frame collapses.

The "no emotion" refusal is risky for brands that *need* emotional connection. Airquity's product is technical; the audience is clinicians and policymakers. If your audience is consumers making emotional purchases, you need emotion.

The "diversity is not the brief" move requires the audience to already accept that diversity is a baseline, not a campaign theme. In markets where diversity is contested, the refusal to use the frame might read as avoidance.
