# Carlsberg — Goal Posters

## What it was trying to do

Return Carlsberg's long-running "If Carlsberg did…" platform, but instead of fantasy football marketing, *make football happen*. The brief was to lower the activation cost of football in cities — turn public space into playable space, and let the brand presence do the talking rather than a 30-second film.

## What it actually did

Installed large green poster-style executions on selected walls in London and Manchester. The posters feature a goal frame. They are not ads — they are *functional goals*. Anyone with a football can use them. No app, no download, no organised event. The brand is the goal; the goal is the brand.

## The tension

**Watching vs. playing.** Beer advertising around football typically celebrates the game through stars, drama, and big-budget storytelling. Carlsberg rejects that. They don't make football more watchable; they make football *playable*. The poster is not a depiction of football — it is the thing you play football with.

A second tension sits underneath: **advertising vs. utility.** A poster is normally a one-way broadcast. Carlsberg's poster accepts input — the ball, the kick, the player. The medium becomes interactive without digital technology.

## Why it works (or doesn't)

It works because the *form* of the poster is the *function* of the brand. Carlsberg sells social occasion. The poster creates social occasion by being literally playable. The cost-of-entry is zero — no download, no permission, no organisation — which is exactly the price point of "casual kickabout."

The category cliché it escapes: **"celebrate football from the stands."** That's the default beer-and-football move — spectatorship, fandom, the hero player. Carlsberg refused that. They gave people a goal, not a story about a goal.

## The visual mechanism

The poster is shaped like a football goal — green panels, white goalposts painted on or shaped into the surface. From a distance it reads as a Carlsberg ad (the brand colour, the "If Carlsberg did…" treatment). Up close, it reads as a goal you can hit. The mechanism is *visual continuity*: an image of a goal and the actual goal share the same form.

Multiple installations across two cities. Some are smaller and tucked into side streets; some are full-wall on main roads. The size determines who plays.

## The typography register

Brand-led. Carlsberg's wordmark is present but restrained — the goal reads first, the brand reads second. There's very little type beyond the brand. The campaign's "If Carlsberg did…" voice is implied in the cleverness rather than spelled out.

## The refusal

Refused: the celebrity endorsement. No star players. No pundits. No fans with painted faces.

Refused: the digital gimmick. No QR code, no app, no AR layer. The poster is the poster.

Refused: the reach-broadcast approach. Beer advertising around football usually wants impressions. Carlsberg wanted interactions. Smaller media budget, more direct participation.

## What to steal

The principle: **make the medium a tool, not a message.** When a brand's brief is to be present in a moment, the strongest execution is one where the medium enables the moment — not just communicates about it. A poster that's also a goal is a stronger play than a poster that's about goals.

A second stealable move: **zero-friction participation.** No app, no download, no QR code. If you have to fight the activation to use it, the activation is wrong. Carlsberg's goal works because any kid with a ball can use it. Frictionless participation is harder than it looks.

A third: **functional brand assets outlast ad creative.** Carlsberg's "If Carlsberg did…" voice has run for years because it offers a structural frame, not a campaign concept. Goal Posters is an *instance* of that frame, not the whole thing. Frame, then instances — not campaigns.

## What not to steal

The "make the poster a goal" move only works where there's space to play. In dense cities where the poster is on a high-traffic pedestrian street with no room to kick a ball, the installation fails. The idea needs a context where playing is possible.

The "skip the digital layer" move is right for football — sport needs bodies, not phones. Don't apply it to categories where digital is the point. A beer brand can skip digital; a fintech cannot.

The functional-asset frame assumes brand longevity. "If Carlsberg did…" has run for 30 years. If your brand voice is annual, the frame won't carry. Frame-first brand work is a multi-year bet, not a campaign.
