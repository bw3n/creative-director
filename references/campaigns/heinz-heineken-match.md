## What it was trying to do

HEINZ and Heineken have shared restaurants, sports events, and family tables for 150 years without ever formally partnering. The brief was to make the existing pairing official — a collaboration that validates behaviour the consumer has been doing for generations.

## What it actually did

A limited-edition six-pack containing five Heineken bottles and one bottle of HEINZ Tomato Ketchup. Supported by a brand film, social content, and packaging. The packaging combines Heineken's green and HEINZ's red-and-white identity into a single design. The campaign borrows the language of sport ("the match we've all been waiting for") to position the pairing as a long-anticipated meeting between two well-known names.

## The tension

**Surprise vs. recognition.** The pairing might look unusual at first — ketchup and beer in the same pack. But most consumers have been ordering both for years. The campaign refuses to introduce the pairing as new; it positions it as *finally official*. The consumer recognises what they've been doing; the brand catch-up validates it.

A second tension sits underneath: **collaboration as novelty vs. collaboration as acknowledgement.** Most brand collaborations introduce a new pair ("Taco Bell × Doritos Locos Tacos"). This one acknowledges an existing pair. Different mechanism, different emotional register.

## Why it works (or doesn't)

It works because the *content* is minimal. There's no new recipe, no new product, no new claim. The campaign's deliverable is the *validation* of an existing behaviour. That's a low-cost, high-trust move.

The category cliché it escapes: **"two brands come together to make a magic new product."** That's the default. HEINZ and Heineken refuse that. The pairing is the point.

## The visual mechanism

The six-pack is the centrepiece. Five green Heineken bottles and one red-and-white HEINZ bottle, packaged together. The visual is a deliberate one-in-six oddity — your eye finds the ketchup bottle because it's different from the others.

The packaging combines both visual identities: Heineken's green with HEINZ's red-and-white. The combination reads as "two brands in one design" rather than one design that swallows the other.

The campaign film uses sport language ("the match we've all been waiting for") — borrowed from football coverage. Football fans read the campaign immediately; non-football fans still get the pairing message.

## The typography register

Both brands' wordmarks are present. HEINZ's wordmark sits in its traditional form; Heineken's in its traditional form. They don't blend or unify — they coexist. The register is "two brands signing together," not "one brand emerging from a merger."

## The refusal

Refused: the new-product frame. The campaign doesn't ask consumers to try a new flavour or use a new recipe. It validates existing behaviour.

Refused: the celebrity endorsement. No footballers, no actors, no influencers.

Refused: the comparison frame. The campaign doesn't compare HEINZ to other ketchups or Heineken to other beers. The focus is on the pairing, not the brands' relative merits.

## What to steal

The principle: **acknowledge existing behaviour rather than introduce new one.** When a brand collaboration has cultural roots already, the campaign's job is to *validate* the behaviour, not to *create* it. The cheapest, most-believable collaborations are ones the consumer has been doing for years.

A second stealable move: **the 1-in-N packaging oddity.** The six-pack with one ketchup bottle is a visual that rewards attention. Your eye finds the odd one out. The visual signal carries the message without a word.

A third: **borrow register from a familiar domain.** "The match we've all been waiting for" borrows football coverage language. The reader doesn't need a glossary; the framing does the work.

## What not to steal

The "validate existing behaviour" move only works when the behaviour exists. If you're manufacturing a pairing the consumer hasn't been doing, you're back to the novelty frame — and you need a real hook.

The packaging-oddity visual works when one item is meaningfully different. If you're putting 50/50 HEINZ and Heineken in a pack, the eye finds no oddity. The asymmetry is the mechanism.

The "borrow sports register" is right for a beer-and-food pairing. Don't borrow sports register for categories where the consumer doesn't speak sports. Don't borrow finance register for consumer goods.
